PREFACE
An independent group of Greek Cypriot, Turkish Cypriot and international academic researchers
are engaged by the Management Centre of the Mediterranean to carry out a research of the
tourism industry on both sides of the Green line to answer the question; what happens to tourism
in both parts of the island with or without a solution.

Tourism industry has traditionally been the backbone of the economy in Cyprus since the
independence in 1960. Even after the separation of the two economies in the north and south
both sides benefited from tourism revenues although the south was in a more advantageous
position being without any political constraints such as lack of political recognition as the north
was experiencing.

In the past few years there is a lot of discussion on both sides about the future of tourism on the
island due to uncertainties and instabilities in the relative sectors. The main themes of these
discussions are first what are the costs and benefits of a divided or reunited tourism industry on
the island?, and second what type of tourism strategy is needed for Cyprus in order to carry the
industry in the next 10-20 years and contribute to the sustainable development of the island?. The
focus of this study has been to find some answers to both of these questions by asking the people
who are active in the industry and have hands on experience and understanding of the situation.

The findings of this particular study suggests that a united tourism strategy based on
special interest tourism will be a more sustainable and profitable way forward rather than
keeping two separate tourism industry on the island dependable on mainly mass tourism.
98% of the Turkish Cypriot respondents and 79% of the Greek Cypriot respondents are
seeing a win-win situation with a joint tourism industry. More than 50% of the TC sees a
20-30% percent increase in their turnover in two years and more than 50% of the GC sees
more than 10% increase in their turnover in three years in case there is a joint effort.

3

The econometric analysis looking at demand sensitivity suggests that high income groups who
are attracted to special interest tourism will be more suitable for a joint tourism industry on the
island

It should be mentioned at this point that the period of the study was before the Presidential
elections in the south held in February 2008. There is no empirical evidence to use in order to
comment about how the mood of people changing towards prospects for a solution after the
elections.
One cannot pretend that these questions can be answered by a single study of this sort but we are
aiming to make a contribution to the better understanding of the situation in order to help the
practitioners in the sector and policy makers to use these findings for more informed decision
making.

I.

INTRODUCTION

This is an empirical study of the all-Cyprus tourism industry, defined as tourist agencies and
hoteliers, in both the North and South, consisting of five principal parts:

I. Introduction
II. Profile of the industry under existing (divided) conditions (the status quo).
The profile covers a statistical description of employment, wages,
profitability, and obstacles as perceived by respondents that create
transactions costs that prevent higher profit margins.
III. A subjective analysis of expectations, fears and hopes of respondents in
the North and South regarding the impact of a possible settlement
acceptable to both communities on potential profits and losses.
IV. An econometric model of future efficiency gains of a possible settlement
for All-Cyprus and the distribution of these gains between the North and
South.
V. Conclusion, which highlights the major findings.

The basic hypothesis of the study is that a settlement acceptable to both communities will result
in significant efficiency gains for all1, or a Win-Win outcome for the entire tourism industry in
the North and South as a result of expansion of foreign demand for holidays in a united Cyprus.
The behavioral assumption here is that foreign tourists, being rational decision-makers carefully
choosing where to holiday, will prefer a united rather than a divided destination. They will have
free and unimpeded access to more historic sites, expanded facilities, more competition, “free
skies” and overall wider choices.

1

Efficiency gains can be defined as “peace dividend” following an agreed settlement between Turkish- and Greek
Cypriots in Cyprus. The peace dividend is expected to be huge. For a recent empirical study of estimated peace
dividend see the study: The Day After: Commercial Opportunities Following a Solution in the Cyprus Problem by
Fiona Mullen, Ozlem Oguz and Praxoula Antoniades Kyriacou, PRIO Cyprus Center Study 1/2008.

5

Equally significantly, unification will enhance prospects of cooperative tourism development
between investors and operators in both parts of the currently divided island. The econometric
model highlights the locations from which this demand is expected by the respondents.

The empirical information on which the study is based is generated by respondents in the tourism
industry. A standard questionnaire was used by the Research Teams in both areas (see Appendix
I). In the North, a total 70 respondents, and a total of 92 in the South, voluntarily provided the
required information during the survey stage of the study. In addition, Key Informant Surveys
were undertaken for more detailed information regarding the econometric forecasting model
used in the study (see below).

II.

PROFILING THE STATUS QUO

In the present divided Cyprus, two separate tourism sectors exist each operating under totally
different, partly non-competing markets. In the North, the presence of the state is dominant in all
facets of tourism, intended to compensate for political isolation the North is experiencing; in the
South, the semi-governmental Cyprus Tourism Organization is responsible for strategic
planning, promoting and regulating the tourism product, under the aegis of the Ministry of
Commerce, Industry and Tourism. While there are direct flights to the airports in the South, this
is not available in the North where flights have to go through Turkey generating significant time
delays and indirect costs for tourists.

In both areas, there is a corresponding publicly owned and managed national airline, Cyprus
Airways for the South and Turkish Cypriot Airlines in the North. Both are high-cost, over-staffed
and loss-making enterprises.

Increasingly, however, customers are taking advantage of competition as a result of freedom of
movement between North and South. Thus, Turkish Cypriots and EU citizens (e.g. British and
German) residing in the North are able to use Larnaca airport, while Greek Cypriots can use
Ercan to fly to Turkey, thereby taking advantage of lower fares (it should be noted, however, that
the use of Ercan by Greek Cypriots is frowned upon by the government).

THE TOURISM INDUSTRY IN THE NORTH

In most of the day-to-day administration, the industry is self-governing through the Association
of Turkish Cypriot Hoteliers and Tourist Agencies. However, the department in charge of
Tourism administers a large Tourism Development Fund, organizing and participating in
international fairs, marketing and publicity for the annual tourism season, which peaks in
summer.

In addition, there is a large investment incentive program for development of additional capacity
in the tourism sector through new hotels and tourism facilities. This incentive program is
administered by the Planning Organization and a certificate of approval provides exemption from
income tax for a stipulated number of years, and duty free imports and other tax privileges to
foreign and domestic investors.

Our survey results indicate that the tourism industry in the North although not stable, none of the
70 respondents suffered declining revenues in the last 12 months, almost four out of every five
made between 10 and 20% higher gross revenues, 17.1% reported revenue increases in excess of
30%, and just three realized only between 5% and 10% increases (Table I).

III. EXPECTATIONS, FEARS AND HOPES IN THE TOURISM
INDUSTRY IN THE EVENT OF A POSSIBLE UNITED CYPRUS
In this Part of the study we shall focus on how a possible settlement of the Cyprus Problem is
expected by respondents to impact the tourism industry. Expectations of likely impact of a
possible settlement were contrasted against the prospect of continuation of the current (divided)
situation. Respondents were asked to give their subjective opinions of the impact of a possible
settlement in terms of the following four options: (i) win-win for both sides, (ii) win for Greek
Cypriots and loss for Turkish Cypriots, (iii) loss for Greek Cypriots and win for Turkish Cypriots
and (iii) loss for both sides.

RESPONDENTS IN THE SOUTH

The Existing Situation (Status Quo) Scenario:
Among Greek Cypriot tourism enterprises, there is a high degree of uncertainty over the future of
the industry if the status quo remains unchanged. There is on the other hand, confidence over the
future in the case of a settlement. Table XVII shows that the most common view was of a
continuation of the current situation leading to a lose-lose impact in both communities. Over a
quarter of the respondents apparently agreed that it would have a negative impact on tourism in
the south, but a positive impact on tourism in the north. This leaves a small but significant one
fifth of respondents with the view that a continuation of the status quo will have a positive
impact on tourism in the south with diverging opinions on what happens to the north.

TRANSACTION COSTS AND EFFICIENCY GAINS
IN A POSSIBLE UNITED CYPRUS
The existing status quo in the tourism industry is high-cost, resulting in high transaction and
operating costs. To give just one example, there are two separate loss-making national airlines,
Cyprus Airways in the South, and Turkish Cypriot Airlines in the North. According to
newspaper reports7, the former airline made a loss of CYP 22.6 million in 2005, a further but
much reduced loss of CYP 4.8 million in 2006, and in 2007 it received a 78 million euro loan
from the Hellenic Bank in order to “improve the economic results of the airline” (Cyprus
Airways recently announced that it made a profit in 2007).

The Turkish Cypriot Airlines (TCA) is a state-owned company. It is also loss-making owing to a
variety of operational and managerial reasons, including in particular over-staffing and lack of
direct flights to major destinations.

These high cost conditions can only be overcome if there is significant expansion of the tourism
industry with substantial foreign demand. Will a political settlement stimulate foreign demand
for Cyprus as a more attractive destination? In theory it should owing to a variety of reasons,
including attractiveness of a unified country and efficiency gains from economies of scale.

Opening Marash/Varosha, now a closed city in ruins, with a pre-1974 capacity of over 10,000
hotel rooms, alone can be expected to provide a huge expansion. The required investment to
create additional capacity will generate a powerful ‘win-win” opportunity for the tourism
industry on both sides.

However there are economic and operational problems, partly management-related, partly
structural. For one thing, there is intense competition amongst airlines. CA, which is stateowned, faces fierce competition from airlines that are privately run and thus have more
flexibility to operate under present conditions. Low cost airlines are also entering the market and

The theoretical model is illustrated above in Fig. I. Panel (A) relates to the existing
divided markets scenario, whereas Panel (B) refers to a unified tourist market in AllCyprus. DN describes foreign tourist demand for the North and similarly DS relates to the
South. In Panel (A), what is significant is the cost differential CS and CN favoring the
North. In the event of an acceptable settlement for both sides, a united Cyprus is expected
to become a more attractive destination and DT0 will shift outwards to DT1 generating
more business in both zones with convergence of costs and prices at MCT.
MCT is the All-Cyprus unit cost (or marginal cost) of delivering service to customers in
the tourism industry. The interaction of DT1 and MCT determine the total tourist arrivals
in Cyprus through the international airport at Larnaca, Paphos and at Ercan and Gecitkale
in the North as well as seaports. In 2006, the total number of tourist arrivals in the North
was about 0.7 million, and the corresponding number for the South was 2.4 million,
reflecting higher capacity in the tourism industry.

Under unified Cyprus, there will be higher foreign demand, but costs and prices will
converge. The additional tourist inflow will be allocated between the North and South,
according to available capacity, with costs and prices equalized. Both sides will benefit,
most of the gains accruing to the South, the larger market. In a united Cyprus there will
be a Win-Win outcome for the tourism industry, leading to efficiency gains.

Is this win-win scenario likely? Tourism industry representatives are in the best position
to answer this question that is, in reality, an empirical one. We attempted to get
information from these representatives in two ways: First, through the responses of
respondents in the surveys. Secondly, we conducted specific Key Informant Surveys for
detailed information from knowledgeable stakeholders of the tourism sector.
Additionally, a special econometric model was formulated and applied for both the North
and the South to identify the most important determinants of international demand for
tourism. We now provide information about this econometric application.

IV. MODELING INTERNATIONAL TOURISM DEMAND FOR
NORTH AND SOUTH CYPRUS (UNDER STATUS QUO)
In this study, we estimated the international tourism demand for the North and South
separately. In the estimation exercise tourist arrivals per capita is the dependent variable
and income per capita, cost of living (proxy for tourist price index) are the independent
variables. The data fitted in the case of the North relate to the three top tourist sending
countries namely, Turkey, the UK, and Germany. In the case of the South, the three top
countries are Greece, the UK, and Germany. In each case actual annual data were used to
make the time series analysis for the period 1980 to 2006.
The Econometric Modelling

Witt and Martin (1987) developed an econometric model for international tourism
demand. We will use a modified version, which is explained below in double logarithmic
form.

In the North case, the statistically significant independent variable explaining variation in
per capita tourist arrival are (in order of importance) national income per head for
Turkish and British tourists and cost of living for British and German tourists. In the case
of the South, national income per head is significant for British tourist only while cost of
living is significant only for Germans. Our estimation model also indicates that repeat
tourism is significant for both the North and the South. In the case of the South the
British are highly significant for repeat tourism while in the North Turkish and German
repeat tourist are significant but to a lesser extent.

Our estimated results suggest that the policy makers must promote high income tourism
product (such as special interest packages) rather than mass tourism for North Cyprus.
Cost of living in the North is the main determinant of the British and German tourists
choosing the North as a holiday destination.

For the South, our results indicate that national income per head is the most important
determinant for the British tourist arrivals, while the cost of living variable is similarly
the most important in the case of German tourist arrivals. It is therefore suggested that the
policy makers in the South must promote special interest tourism rather than mass
tourism for the British, while keeping in mind that German holiday-makers are especially
sensitive to the cost of living in the South. The Greek travel agents and hotel owners
should be particularly sensitive to the price sensitivity of the German tourist market.

V. CONCLUSIONS AND HIGHLIGHTS
All Cyprus

Revenue and Profitability under Status Quo: In the present (divided) context, tourism
industry appears to be on rising revenue trend overall but not stable and sustainable, and
it is generally felt that profitability will increase with a settlement.

Short-run Pessimism, Long Run Optimism: Two major findings stand out in this
study: (1) Pessimism is dominant on both sides if the present (divided) situation remains
in force, suggesting lost business opportunities and growth for the tourism industry; and
(2) higher profits and optimistic prospects in the event of an acceptable settlement to both
sides, suggesting a Win-Win outcome.

However, a quick settlement does not appear to be the dominant view, indicating the need
for a significant transition period for healing and reconciliation leading to future
cooperation in the industry. Particularly in the South, there is widespread resentment over
property previously owned by Greek Cypriots but currently used by Turkish Cypriots.
Related to this fact, there is also a general reluctance to acknowledge the major
investments that current users of properties in the north have undertaken to improve
properties in order to enhance the final product on offer in the north. Extensive education
and reconciliation is required to overcome the widespread hostility which is likely to bar
future cooperative projects in tourism between Greek- and Turkish Cypriots businessmen.

More competition & Choice: In the event of an acceptable political settlement, there
will be greater competition in the tourism industry on the island between existing
stakeholders (airlines, hotels, restaurants and transport providers). There will also be
greater choice available to tourists within easy access. As a result, two economic
consequences are predictable: (1) convergence of prices and costs in All-island tourism,
and (2) winners who will be able to provide tourists price- and quality-competitive
services, and losers who will cease to exist because they will be unable to compete.

37

`

End of State Subsidies: Almost certainly the North and South will all be effectively EU
territory subject to the same rules and conditions under the same acquis communitaires If,
or to the extent, that this would mean zero-public subsidies, the survival of tourist
enterprises in the island will very much depend on one’s ability to compete.

Settlement will Boost Business: A substantial majority of respondents in the North and
South are optimistic about the impact of an acceptable settlement, expecting foreign
demand to expand in that event. Interestingly in the South, Turkey is regarded as a
potential new market8.

Recommendations

Admittedly this is a limited study. The empirical findings, however, are significant and
support the following recommendations.

Promote Reconciliation: To achieve the Win-Win outcome in the longer-term which
this study has highlighted empirically, it is necessary to promote reconciliation in the
tourism industry on both sides by a carefully planned follow-up action that would bring
progressive minded industry stakeholders together so they can undertake cooperative and
joint tourism development projects. The first step in this direction might be a series of
carefully organized seminars facilitated by academics and supported by international
expertise.

Such seminars can play a vital springboard role for reconciliation overcoming sensitive
issues (e.g. property previous owners and investment by current owners) and result in
joint brochures, advertising and marketing plans. However, tangible results are required
for active promotion of reconciliation in order cash in the longer-term Win-Win scenario
underlined by the present findings. These tangible results include promotion of joint
marketing, including politically correct brochures, publicity and business practices.

Phase Out “Enemy” Image: It is recommended that education and publicity campaign
are launched by officials and industry bodies, as well as on a voluntary basis by tourism
businesses themselves, to replace the current “enemy” image of the other side. In doing
so, a policy of “Correct Tourism” would seek to achieve neutrality and thus would among
other things, refrain from stopping foreign tourists from crossing the green line.

Ensure Freedom of Movement and Travel: All obstacles and impediments for Allisland travel in tourism, including in transport, travel and advertising in North-South
tourism. “Free Skies” is now an official EU policy designed to stimulate greater
competition in airlines business.

Reduce Transaction Costs with Competition and Merit System: Especially in the
North, there is excessive government control of the industry causing high transaction and
operating costs. To reduce these costs, more privatization and self-government of the
tourism sector (e.g. by creating a Tourism Board) is a pre-condition for a leaner, more
competitive environment. In personnel policy, political appointments need to be replaced
by professionalism and merit.

APPENDIX II: METHODOLOGY AND SOURCES OF THE
ECONOMETRIC MODEL
For the dependent variable, we collected the tourist arrivals in nominal total
disaggregated value. Then, we divided them by the population of the origin country to
convert the number of arrival figures into per capita form. This is done in order to
eliminate multicollinearity problems, which may be caused by population and number of
arrivals as explained above. After we obtained the per capita number of arrivals from
origin countries’ to destination countries’, we took the logarithms in order to obtain the
various elasticity measures.

Income data (INC) are generated from GDP per capita figures, and are collected from
the World Bank- World Economic Indicators.
The cost of living variable10 (COL) was used as a proxy for the consumer price index
since COL gives better explanations in econometric understandings.

The CPI

(2000=100) of the destination country was divided by the effective exchange rate (EXR)
of the origin country. The result obtained was divided by the CPI of the origin country.
Everything was processed in index format. Then, the logarithm was taken in order to
obtain the elasticities from the regression.

Break down dummies were also included into the regression for countries of origin to
measure the effects of the 1991 Gulf crisis. All will take value 1 for the indicated year
and 0 otherwise.

We used lagged dependent variables (Aij(t-1)/P j(t-1)) to discover whether demand is
supply constrained, and also to represent habit persistence (whereby people tend to return
to a place they know). As word of mouth plays a very important role in choosing a
holiday destination, one good (or bad) holiday experience at the destination can have
considerable longer-term knock-on effects. Another reason for the inclusion of a lagged
dependent variable in tourism demand functions comes from the supply side. Witt and

Witt (1992) emphasized that supply constraints may take the form of shortages of hotel
accommodation, passenger transportation capacity and trained staff, and these often
cannot be increased rapidly. Time is also required to build up contacts among tour
operators, hotels, airlines and travel agencies. Witt (1980) also pointed out that only a
gradual decline in a country’s popularity is at all likely, since investment in hotel
accommodation will have already taken place and tour operators will have established
contacts there. In addition, tour operators need time to adjust to changing patterns of
inclusive holiday demand. Witt (1980) and Gujarati (1988) indicated that if a partial
adjustment mechanism is postulated to allow for rigidities in supply, this results in the
presence of a lagged dependent variable in the tourism demand model (Yorucu, 1998).

Morley (2000) pointed out the problem of spurious regression, which is the consequence
of running regressions with non-stationary data. The stationarity is therefore necessary in
order to avoid the unreliable and misleading parameter estimates.

Order of integration and unit root tests

Dickey-Fuller (DF) (1979, 1981) unit root tests or alternatively Augmented Dickey Fuller
(ADF) tests with appropriate lag values are the general tests for stationarity. Hendry and
Erricson (1990) general to specific modelling is a common approach on stationarity tests.
In this study we used unit root tests suggested by, Dickey and Fuller (1981) and Phillips
and Perron (1988).

Cointegration analysis

Evidence of the same order of integration is required for the test of cointegration and
Granger-causality. Cointegration analysis shows us the long-run equilibrium relationship
between annual tourist arrivals to North Cyprus from Turkey, the UK, and Germany as
well as the tourist arrivals to South Cyprus from Greece, the UK, and Germany and the
factors that influence arrivals. In order to be able to estimate the long-run relationship
between these variables, they must have common trends and must move together in the
45

`

long run. In other words, all variables must have been found non-stationary from the unit
root tests. If there is a cointegration, then an error correction model (ECM) of tourist
arrivals must be constructed and estimated by taking the first differences of the series and
taking the first lag of the residuals from an auxiliary regression. An ECM will overcome
the problem of spurious regression associated with traditional econometric modelling
approaches to estimating tourism demand functions.

We used Johansen and Juselius (1990) maximum likelihood estimation technique to test
the cointegration relationship. The results are illustrated in Table-4 below.

The cointegrating equations were used to estimate the long-run equilibrium relationships
between tourist arrivals and factors that influence these arrivals such as income and cost
of living. The Johansen and Juselius (1990) test statistics can be used to determine the
number of statistically significant long-run relationship between tourist arrivals and the
factors that influence the arrivals. We tested for the possibility of multiple long-run
relationships using the Johansen (1991) test. Table-4 provides log-likelihood ratio
statistics for determining the number (r) of long-run relationships between quarterly
tourist arrivals, income, and cost of living. The number of the long-run relationships is
determined sequentially starting with the null hypothesis r= 0 which is no long-run
relationship between quarterly tourist arrivals, income, and cost of living. To reject the
null of r= 0 (no long-run relationship) in favour of the alternative that r= 1, the calculated
value of the statistics should be greater than the 5% critical value. The results tabulated
above indicates that the null of r= 0 (no long-run relationship between tourist arrivals,
income, and cost of living) can be rejected in favour of the alternative r= 1. For example,
Turkey has one statistically significant long-run relationship between tourist arrivals,
income, and cost of living. Critical values were derived assuming no exogenous series. In
short, for equation 1 to 3, Turkey (1981-2006), UK, Germany, (1986-2006) models have
one statistically significant long-run relationship between tourist arrivals, income and cost
of living. For equations 4 to 6, Greece, UK, and Germany (1983-2006) models also have
one statistically significant long-run relationship between tourist arrivals, income and cost
of living. In all cases the null of r 0 can be rejected in favour of the alternative r= 1; this

tourist arrivals, income, and cost of living. Whereas, the null of r= 0, r 1, r 2, cannot be
rejected to the alternative r= 1, r= 2, r= 3, correspondingly (at 5% critical value).

The estimated parameters for Turkey, UK, Germany and Greece mostly have expected
signs for income and for cost of living. All income coefficients exceed unity for equations

positive but demand is income inelastic (0.35) for Greece since it is lower than 1.
Kulendran (1996) stated that more distant destinations generally have high-income
elasticities.

Since we used cost of living as a proxy for consumer price index, the cost of living
coefficients give us the price elasticities. The coefficient signs for price variables were
found as expected for Turkey (eq 1), the UK (eq 2) and Germany (eq 6) only. The tstatistics for cost of living coefficients are found to be significant for Turkey and the rest
but insignificant for Germany.

Short-run tourism demand dynamic modelling (Error Correction Mechanism)

Econometric theory simply emphasise that a group of non-stationary time series is
cointegrated if there is a linear combination of them that is stationary; that is, the
combination does not have a stochastic trend. The linear combination is called the
cointegrating equation. Its normal interpretation is a long-run equilibrium relationship. If
you have N endogenous variables, of which each is first-order integrated I(1) series; that
is, each has a unit root or stochastic trend or random-walk element), there can be from
zero to N-1 linearly independent cointegrating vectors.

The series that we used for the Johansen cointegration tests were non-stationary series.
For normal tests we used 2 lag variables and we found at least one cointegrated equation

at 5% significance level. The standard time series analysis such as VAR11 applied first
differences of the data. Because there are N separate integrated elements driving the
series, levels of the series did not appear in the VAR. If there was one cointegrating
equation, the VAR would need an error correction term involving levels of the series, and
this term would appear on the right-hand side of each of the VAR equations, which
otherwise would be in the first differences. Each additional cointegrating equation
contributes another error correction term involving levels of the series on the right-hand
side of each VAR equation. For all equations we implemented an error correction
estimation with different lag levels.
We can see consistencies in the income variable, since it is income elastic (above unity)
at one lag level. However, for Germany’s case the error correction result is not consistent
with the cointegration result. It is income inelastic (below unity), yet, it is not statistically
significant. All equations have significant t-values at one lag level except Germany.
Concerning the cost of living variable, the error correction estimation indicates less
consistent results than cointegration for Turkey; which is price inelastic (0.001) at one
lag level and found insignificant (0.04). For the UK the coefficient is (-0.127) which is
price inelastic at one lag levels and for Germany it is (-0.102) which are price inelastic at
one lag level. The error correction estimation gave us satisfactory t-values for all
equations at 5% significance level. The coefficient signs for all variables in all equations
were found to be the expected signs except for Turkey. Strong consistencies were found
between the cointegration results and the error correction estimation for most of the
variables.
In South Cyprus case, if we compare the error correction results with the cointegration
results, for Greece, Uk and Germany models, we can see inconsistencies in the income
variable, since income inelastic (below unity) results are obtained for Greece and the UK
at one lag level. However, for Germany’s case the error correction result is found to be
income inelastic (above unity) but statistically is not significant. The t-values at one lag
level which shows the significance of the test shows that only the equation for UK
indicates meaningful elasticity coefficient (0.448).
