Picks for kicks

If you're considering getting involved in martial arts,
there are wide variety from which to choose — unless you
live in Two Dot.

In Missoula, the University of Montana harbors under
its wing clubs that offer karate, taekwondo, aikido, tai chi
and judo classes tirough the Center for Continuing Educa-
tion. A private school, Samurai, 1290 S. Third St. W., also
has a martial arts program and self-defense classes.

Prices for UM center courses average about $15 for a
quarter, with three to four and a half hours of classes each
week. Samurai charges about $45 a month for about four
hours of training each week.

Equipment is cheap: A pair of white ‘‘pajamas,”’
called gi (pronounced ghee by the initiated), is sold by the
UM clubs for about $15. Weapons such as staffs or wooden
swords run to about $8.

Although descriptive in Asian languages, the names of
martial arts don’t mean much to Westerners, so it’s useful
to observe different classes before selecting one.

Each teacher has his own style, in keeping with the
tradition of martial arts that were often perfected by indi-
viduals in warlords’ castles, schools or monasteries.

The arts and weapons used reflect lifestyles and his-
torical periods: For example, nunchakus — two sticks
linked by a chain — are nothing more than rice huskers
used by Okinawa peasants to fight off Japanese invaders.

The differences between the major Asian arts are tied
to the countries where they were developed.

From Japan come kendo, art of the sword; aikido,
which emphasizes self-defense; jujitsu, open-hand combat,
and judo. Judo is a form of jujitsu, but it includes rules for
competition that were developed to keep contestants from
killing each other.

Chinese monasteries produced kung fu — or gung fu
— a generic term used by Westerners to refer to Chinese
martial arts. Techniques — for example “grasping the tail
of the swallow” and ‘‘moving the buffalo through water”
— imitate the movements of animals that masters adapted
to fit man’s methods of combat. Mental training is consid-
ered more important than the physical techniques.

Also from China comes tai chi, the national exercise
form. Techniques are intricate, with sometimes more than
140 steps, and the art resembles aikido.

The development of karate illustrates how the arts in-
fluenced each other: The major styles of karate — empty-
hand fighting — were developed in Japan, Okinawa, China
and Korea. Taekwando is the name of the Korean karate
technique.

According to a catalog of martial arts, Japanese styl-
ists tend to move in linear patterns, and many Japanese-
style techniques are formed by straight lines.

Okinawans, who perfected their karate from the Chi-
nese kung fu, added a circular pattern of blocking tech-
niques.

And a person practicing taekwando can be identified
readily because of the emphasis on spectacular kicks.

Chinese techniques are smoother and include many
low stances and circular hand patterns.

In all martial arts, a black belt indicates that the stu-
dent has completed the basic cycle of training and is ready
to start learning the art. Most arts have 10 black-belt levels,
but those become honorary after the fifth or sixth level.

Different color belts are used, mostly in Western coun-
tries, to mark a student’s progress between white and black
belt. Color belts often have two levels, or kyu.

In karate, belts go from white to yellow, green, blue,
brown and black. The colors are symbolic: yellow for the
shoot that sprouts from the earth and turns green under
the sun, on its way to becoming a tree; blue to represent
the young tree that can support itself but still sways in the
wind; brown to show the tree is getting stronger and starts
looking like a tree; and black as the tree gains maturity:
Outward transformations are over. Now it will grow in size.

Belts are not important, because wherever you are,
you have to continue to grow, says Bob Czaja, a UM aikido
student. But, he adds, how many people would go to col-
lege if there was no diploma at the end?

Still it can be done like Bruce Lee. ‘‘I don’t have any
belt whatsoever,” he once said. ‘Unless you can really do
it, that belt doesn’t mean anything. I think it might be use-
ful to hold your pants up.”
