Clearing a path to wide-open spaces

If you're a backcountry skier, you know the
toughest part of your Sierra adventure isn’t al-
ways the trek to the top of the hill. Sometimes,
the real test occurs before you start kicking and
gliding. Namely, the search for a parking place.

The state-run Sno-Park program has helped
solve much of the parking crunch. Now in its
third season, it provides snow-cleared spaces in
many popular winter recreation areas.

A $10 season permit or a $2 day pass gives you
access to designated sites in the Sierra Nevada.
Pick one up at ski shops, California State Auto-
mobile Association offices and assorted other
outlets throughout Northern California. (To re-
ceive a season permit by mail, send $10 to: Sno-
Park, P.O. Box 942896, Sacramento 94296-0001.)

Here's a Sno-Park rundown in the northern Si-
erra:

¢ Yuba Pass: South side of Highway 49 at
Yuba Pass in Sierra County. Since snowmobilers
usually follow routes south of the highway, offi-
cials urge cross-country skiers to concentrate on
the north side.

© Donner Summit: Castle Peak exit of Inter-
state 80; proceed on the south-side frontage road
beyond Boreal Motel and park on the left.

Ski touring routes lie in Castle Valley north of
I-80 (but, authorities warn, “Beware of ava-
lanche and rock fall on the steeper slopes”). A
loading-unloading area at the trailhead helps
minimize pedestrian traffic under the freeway.

¢ Eagle Lakes: Interstate 80, Eagle Lakes
Road north. The snow in this area is reportedly
best after a heavy storm.

¢ Blackwood Canyon: Three miles south of
Tahoe City, west of Highway 89 off Barker Pass

Picturing the earth — “Photographic
equipment and techniques,” outdoor photogra-
pher Freeman Patterson says, “are to a picture
what a car or airplane is to the trip — they get us
there, but they are not the reason for our jour-
ney.”

In his new large-format book, “Portraits of
Earth” (Sierra Club, $35), Patterson explores the
elements, patterns and designs found in the out-
doors, revealing nature’s many moods as the light
and seasons change.

More than 100 beautifully reproduced photos
portray graphic, and often sensual, qualities of”
the earth’s features: sand dunes, ice floes, the
crest of a wave, reflections in a mountain lake. He
calls such pictures “earthscapes.”

Each of four collections of images is preceded
by an essay on his overall vision — including com-
position, camera position, lens choice and ways in
which spectacular effects of wilderness photogra-
phy can be achieved close to home.

Patterson lives in New Brunswick, Canada, and
travels around the United States giving photo
seminars. His previous books include three excel-
lent guides: “Photography for the Joy of It,” “Pho-
tography and the Art of Seeing” and “Photography
of Natural Things.”

“The person who cares about Earth,” he writes
in his new book, “is likely to observe it thoughtful-
ly, to see patterns of relationships between things
that a casual observer does not. The photographs
of Earth that I like best are like portraits of an old
friend. They reveal the essence of Earth itself.”
