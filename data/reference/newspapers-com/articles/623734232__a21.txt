Have you ever gone to a race
around Sacramento or the Bay area
and noticed a bunch of runners
standing around the finish line wear-
ing club singlets?

Do you wonder who they are?
What the club is like? If you could
join?

In San Francisco, you'll spot these
runners in Pamakid, Tamalpa and
Impala gear, while farther down the
Peninsula you’ll likely see a few
West Valley Track Club shirts.

Reno has its Silver State Striders
and in Sacramento, it’s either the
Buffalo Chips or the Pacific Flyers.

All of these are non-profit clubs
whose purpose, in one way or anoth-
er, is to further the interests of run-
ners of all stripes, from those who
just go out for an occasional jog to
the speedy, post-collegiate athlete
who needs a source for information
on top races and training.

In the case of the Sacramento
clubs, the Chips is the most broad-
based, while the Flyers caters al-
most exclusively to the elite runner,
kind of like the Aggie Running Club
in the Bay area.

Since the typical Sacramento run-
ner is probably a candidate for the
Chips, here’s some information on
the club:

The Chips have been going
through growing pains for a few
years — including a major change in
its board of directors — and appears
to be emerging from its humble be-
ginnings into a viable organization
capable of leading Sacramento’s di-
verse running community.

Today the Chips have more than
500 members, up by over 100 in the
last year. Its biggest draw is the
Tuesday workout, started in 1980
and open to members and non-mem-
bers. The workout begins at Steve’s
Pizza on Howe Avenue at 5:30 p.m.

Dave Ragsdale, the club’s presi-
dent, says the club is a “service to
people interested in all levels of run-
ning.”

The Tuesday workout is a prime
example. After some opening an-
nouncements, the gathering is bro-
ken down into groups composed of
two levels: Beginners, who run three
or four slow miles on the American
River bike trail, and distance run-
ners, who do about nine miles, much

The club also has a Thursday
workout, at 5:30 p.m., which is con-
ducted at the California State Uni-
versity, Sacramento, track.

And on weekends the club cur-
rently is conducting “Rescue” runs.
The name refers to a town that’s
near the start of these runs near Fol-
som Lake. The runs, which vary in
length from 10 to 17 miles, are
aimed at people preparing for the
California International Marathon in
December or other longer events.

Besides the workouts, the club
sponsors four yearly community
running events on certified courses:
the Jed Smith 50; the Summer 10K in
Land Park in July; the Susan B. An-
thony 5K (the city’s only women’s
only run); and the Buffalo Stainpede
10-miler (the club’s premier event,
coming up Sept. 20).

Members also get six newsletters
and can work on three types of in-
centive awards: Earning a club sin-
glet by improving racing times by 5
percent; earning a special white sin-
glet by meeting club-specified times
at various distances; and earning a
club jacket by putting in volunteer
hours.

The club is in the process of be-
coming a non-profit corporation and
has invested heavily in computer
equipment so it can put on high-qual-
ity running events.

“We're in a metro area now; it’s a
lot more complicated than when the
Chips began. You can’t just draw a
line on the road any more,” Rags-
dale said of road racing.

The biggest shift Ragsdale has not-
ed in the club is its growing number
of women members. They currently
make up half the membership.
Membership costs $5 yearly for
those 21 and younger, $10 for those
over 21 and $13 for a family.

He says the toughest part of put-
ting on a race is finding volunteers.
Persons with questions about the
club should call Ragsdale at 922-
6199.

Milt Whaley is a Sacramento journalist and

runner whose column appears on alternate
Wednesdays in The Great Outdoors.
