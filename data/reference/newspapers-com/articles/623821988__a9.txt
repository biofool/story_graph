When noted Berkeley mountaineer Arlene
Blum returns to Sacramento next week, she'll be
talking and walking, not climbing.

Her slide-lecture program, titled “The Great Hi-
malayan Traverse,” will cover her nine-month,
2,000-mile traverse across the top of the world —
through Bhutan, Nepal and northern India. She
made the epic adventure with Hugh Swift, a veter-
an trek leader from Berkeley.

Their route, she says, led them “through a di-
versity of peoples, cultures, plants and animals
that is unsurpassed anywhere on this planet.”

The show takes place Thursday, Oct. 22, at 7:30
p.m. in the Sacramento City College auditorium,
3835 Freeport Blvd. Admission is $5.

Sponsored by the Sierra Club's Mother Lode
Chapter, the 90-minute program will feature twin
projectors, local music taped during the trek and
Blum’s live narration.

Blum is widely known as the leader of the 1978
American Women’s Himalayan Expedition,
which made history as the first Americans — and
the first women — to scale the perilous slopes of
Annapurna. However, the triumph soon became
tragedy when two climbers on the second summit
team fell to their deaths

That group also will be remembered for Blum’s
compelling book: “Annapurna: A Woman's Place,”
published by Sierra Club Books. (In a previous
Sacramento visit, Blum presented a well-received
slide show on her Annapurna experiences. )

The Great Himalayan Traverse began near the
eastern border of Bhutan, “a smal] Buddhist king-
dom virtually untouched by outside influence.”
Blum and Swift were the first Westerners to re-
ceive permission to walk across this remote land.

the trekkers “crossed icy passes up to 19,000 feet

Modern tents — Ten years ago, most back-
pack shelters came in one shape: the A-frame. To-
day, domes, hoops and other high-tech designs
rule the market. And the trend continues.

Moss Tents’ latest style, for example, is the Syd-
ney family tent — patterned and named after
Australia’s famed opera house, It arches graceful-
ly to a point and features a high-low design, which
allows for leg stretching and standup room. Rear,
eye-shaped windows combine with the front
screen to increase ventilation.

The Sydney sleeps a family of four, weighs in at
13 pounds and retails for $625 (including a life-
time guarantee).

Sierra solitude — “If you haven't found the
peaceful High Sierra solitude John Muir so rev-
eled in over a century ago,” says Jeffrey P. Schaf-
fer, “perhaps you've been visiting the wrong parks
and wildernesses of the Sierra Nevada.”

So what’s the right place? Try the canyons and

valleys of Carson-Iceberg Wilderness, a lightly
used, yet easily accessible area between High-
ways 4 and 108.

For the first time, a hiker’s guidebook to this
region is available. “Carson-Iceberg Wilderness,”
written by Schaffer, a mountaineering naturalist
and a veteran guidebook author, has just been
published by Wilderness Press. It costs $12.95.

On any given summer night, he says, most back-
packers occupy small portions of the 251-square-
mile wilderness, barely amounting to two square
miles, These campsites border Sword, Lost, Rock,
Bull Run, Heiser and Asa lakes, and are around
Soda Springs Guard Station, deep in the canyon of
the East Fork Carson River.

Moderately used campsites exist at several oth-
er places. “That,” he says, “leaves most of the wil-
derness — about 98 percent of it — for seekers of
solitude.”

The book describes established hiking trails,
cross-country exploring, the area's natural histo-
ry, the best places to fish, and the climbing and
mountaineering routes for all of the major peaks.
“Carson-Iceberg” also includes photos and an up-
dated topo map.

(The Carson-Iceberg, by the way, escaped the
Sierra’s recent rash of fires, forestry officials re-
port. The big Stanislaus National Forest blaze,
which charred more than 100,000 acres, struck
southwest of the wilderness.)

Kerry Drager is a freelance journalist who writes
about camping, hiking, skiing and exploring the
