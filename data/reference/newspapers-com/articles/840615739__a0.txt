Sequoia District students find
no ‘Dr. Welby’ in health training

By OTTO TALLENT

Students from three high
schools in the Sequoia Union High
School District are learning that
health care is a far cry from that
depicted on television hospital
shows.

Thirteen students—seven from
Menlo-Atherton, five from Wood-
side and one from Sequoia—are
participating in a comprehensive
medical career program coordin-
ated by Sequoia Hmspital in Red-
wood City in conjunction with the
school district. This 10th annual
program started March 27 and will
conclude June 6.

Stanley Wilson of Sequoia Hos-
pital’s community relations office
said all of the students plan hospi-
tal or medical-related careers, and
this experience aids hem in chart-
ing their professional futures.

The hospital field program is an
extension of a course entitled “In-
troduction to Medical Careers,”
which is taught by Paul Castoro, a
member of the Menlo-Atherton
faculty.

Students attend seminars and
rotate through various depart-
ments, where staff members deter-
mine daily activities. Those may
include observation or perform-
ance of auxiliary hospital duties
Within the students’ capabilities,
Wilson said.

Wilson said the students absorb
fundamental, practical informa-
tion.

Dr. Mark Congress, chairman of
the Emergency Department at the
hospital, told students, “What you
see here is a far cry from the
stories that you may have seen on
the ‘Dr. Welby’ or ‘General Hospi-
tal’ shows,

“Here we deal on a daily basis
with everything from nose bleeds,
to heart attacks, to cuts and
bruises, to gunshot wounds. And
we don't solve all of their problems
in 50 minutes. However, we deliver
good emergency care and get the
patients ready to be treated fur-
ther by their own physicians, or to
return home.”

Nancie Jubitz, speech therapist,
said it is a long trail back before
the patient can utter the simplest
words. That return is punctuated
by frustration, anger, tears, repeti-
tion upon repetition of therapy
sessions, a lot of patience, and fi-
nally, a measure of victory when
the patient correctly pronounces a
word or reads aloud a simple sen-
tence.

Wilson points out that one of the
benefits of the students’ hospital
exposure is personal involvement.

Sue Crawford, a Woodside sen-
ior, made this observation after
attending Ms. Jubitz’s seminar:

“T was really inspired. It’s amaz-
ing what handicapped people can
do with a little help and motiva-
tion. I’m going to call her (Ms.
Jubitz) and see if I can spend some
more time with her and the pa-
tients. I want to find out all I can

about helping that kind of handi-
capped person.”

Several students in the program
have enrolled in evening classes
conducted by hospital personnel.
One such course deals with alter-
nate methods of dealing with fam-
ily stress that is caused by alcoho-
lism and other addictions.

All the students in the program
have completed a course and were
certified in CPR (cardio-pulmo-
nary resuscitation) and also re-
ceived instruction in first aid,

Castoro, who was one of the
program's organizers 10 years ago,
said that over the years the stu-
dents’ grades have improved mea-
surably with the hospital experi-
ence. A few grades have risen
dramatically.

“The hospital provides unique
incentives and motivation,” Cus-
toro said. “The students see very
practical applications of the les-
sons that we have learned in
class.”
