On Martial Arts

artial arts, which originat

ed in ancient China, are to.

tal systems of training of
mind, body and spirit. Some martial
arts, like Karate, emphasize the ex-
ternal form (strength, speed, and
technique), and others, like T’ai Chi,
emphasize the internal form
(breathing, development of aware-
ness and correct core body posture).
But all of the arts ultimately com-
bine the two. To learn a martial art
is to train the mind and body as one
being.

The following books, all review
ed by Whole Earth Review staffers
who practice a martial art, were
chosen to introduce curious readers
into the martial arts. They are no
substitute for a teacher. In fact,
many of the essential concepts of
martial arts can only be learned
through direct experience. But if
you're confused about what martial
arts are or which one might be the
best for you to study, these books
should help.

The Spiritual Dimension

‘a Arts: The Spiritual Di-
mension” helped revive my
love for the martial arts — so much
so that I resumed my Shaolin kung
fu training again after a five-year
break. (I had studied kung-fu for
seven years before.)

The special strength of this
book lies in its clear explanations of
the internal — spiritual and psycho:
logical — aspects of martial arts
that are at the core of serious train-
ing. Author Peter Payne has a
knack for lucidly explaining these
essential concepts that are often dif:
ficult for a beginner (especially a
Westerner) to understand.

a

Another big plus for the book is
Payne's nonchauvinistic stance: No-
where does he proclaim that one
system is better than another. And
he tries to make the reader under-
stand that it is “... best not to cling
to any one position, as sooner or
later you'll be beaten.” Advice well-
taken in general.

The book also introduces basic
techniques such as grappling vs. hit-
ting, hard vs. soft, and empty hands
vs. weapons. If you've ever wonder-
ed what the differences and similar-
ities are between, Jiu-jitsu, Aikido
and Karate, for example, Payne's
broad descriptions will clear your
confusion and help steer you to the
discipline most suited to your per-
sonality and goals.

Almost one-half of this beauti-
fully produced book is photographs
(color and black-and-white) and
drawings that further illustrate
martial arts themes like grounding,
ki, weapon use, “no mind” and heal-
ing. Unfortunately there’s no index,
but the bibliography is excellent

— Candida Kutz-Evelegh

Aikido

eshiba Morihei, the founder of

the Japanese martial art Aiki-
do, repeatedly told his students that
this art was not for harming any-
one, but to provide “loving reconcil-
iation” in all situations of conflict.
Its noncompetitiveness, however,
doesn't keep Aikido from being an
effective form of self-defense.

Aikido is a training in the unifi-
cation of body, mind and spirit that
inevitable spills over into our every-
day lives. Richard Heckler's “Aikido
and the New Warrior” is an antholo-
gy of writings from several men and
women who have applied Aikido’s
principles to their personal and pro-
fessional lives.

“Aikido; The Way of Harmony”
by John Stevens is a good source for
the history, philosophy, and tech-
nique of Aikido. With excellent
black-and-white photographs.

Tai Chi

‘ai Chi emphasizes complete re-

laxation, suppleness and expan-
ded awareness. Through the contin-
uous flowing movements of the
form the practitioner learns to ac-
cumulate and use his or her internal
power, or chi. Lao-Tzu said, “The
soft and pliable will defeat the hard
and strong.” “Not receiving force
{of the attacker] is controlled by me
and is the substance of the art,” T’ai
Chi master Cheng Man Ch’'ing ex-
plains. Although T’ai Chi move-
ments are based on self-defense,
their application is the harnessing
of inner powers. The awareness,
strength and flexibility the practice
cultivates are essential qualities in
any self-defense situation and are
the basis of all martial arts. They're
also essential to a healthy life,
which is why T’ai Chi is suitable for
people of any age.

Chungliang Al Huang teaches
T’ai Chi by emphasizing the princi-
ples of the art over the form. His
book, “Embrace the Tiger, Return
to the Mountain,” is an inspiring
introduction to the spiritual dimen-
sions of T’ai Chi in easy to under-
stand language.

Tai Chi master Cheng Man
Ching’s “Thirteen Treatises” pre-
sents T’ai Chi as a martial art, a
medicine, and a means of exercise '
and self-development. Photographs
and explanations of each move take
you through the whole form.

— Jeanne Carstensen
