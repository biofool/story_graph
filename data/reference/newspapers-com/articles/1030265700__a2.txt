There are many opportunities to work
with children but before considering a
career with children you should ask
yourself a few questions. Do you or did
you enjoy baby-sitting, coaching a chil-
dren's sports team or donating time as a
counselor or tutor? Do you genuinely
enjoy participating in activities with chil-
dren and helping them learn new skills?
Do you like to talk to and listen to chil-
dren? Are you a good role model? Have
you talked to people who work with
children about their careers?

Once you decide that you are the
type of person who enjoys working with
children and you feel you can positively
affect their lives, there are many differ-
ent opportunities to choose from. Some
require a professional degree or license
while others simply require a warm heart
and a good ear. There are always the
more traditional careers such as teacher,
librarian, childcare worker, counselor,
pediatrician and social worker, among
others. But you can also combine your
love or passion, say for photography or
cooking or the martial arts, with your
commitment to working with children.

One woman who has devoted most
of her career to working with children is
Kay O'Connell, Children’s Services Man-
ager at the Sno-Isle Regional Library Sys-
tem. She says she likes working with
children because they are “straightfor-
ward, creative, natural learners and nat-
ural teachers.” A co-worker at Sno-Isle
recently remarked that she couldn't
believe Kay got paid for sitting on the
floor and laughing. “Kids are shameless.
When | was a baby-sitter (they'd) give
me the Sears catalog to read to them so
they could stay up late.”

O'Connell feels that it is important to
work for children. “In the arenas where
decisions are made, children are not ade-
quately represented," she said. She
believes that to work successfully with
children you should be flexible, open to
experiences and have a good sense of
humor. “The rewarding part about work-
ing with kids is that | get to support pro-
grams that promote children's literacy,
growth and development, and have fun
doing it."

Working with children doesn't always
require a college degree. Maybe you just
want to work with kids part-time, say as
a volunteer with the local YWCA or Big
Brothers/Big Sisters of America. Or
maybe you want to start out as an aide
at a child care center. Sport and recre-
ation centers need coaches and play-
ground attendants and tutors to help
with kids. A good source of information
on these and other careers is found in
"Careers for Kids at Heart & Others
Who Adore Children,” by Marjorie
Eberts and Margaret Gisler (VGM Career
Horizons, 2000).

Another path to take is to combine an
interest or hobby with a desire to work
with kids. Joanne Veneziano, owner and
chief instructor of Emerald City Aikido in
Seattle, began her career in social ser-
vices. One day, her aikido instructor
asked her if she could take over the chil-
dren's classes. Veneziano had no prior
experience working with children but she
soon learned how to tune into what
their needs were.

“| learned a lot about teaching aikido
from teaching kids... they learn through
play and that is when | began using
games to help them develop physical
skills.”

Veneziano now teaches eight chil-
dren's classes a week. She says she really
enjoys working with children because
she can contribute significantly to their
lives.

“Lrun into former students in their
twenties and they tell me what they got
out of taking my classes,” she said.
“Children teach me a lot as well... their
job is play and it keeps me playful.”

Whether you are seeking a profes-
sional career working with children or
you just want to spend a few hours a
week teaching a child the guitar or soft-
ball, the rewards of working with chil-
dren are significant. As Veneziano point-
ed out, not only do you get to retain a
sense of play, but you are also able to
positively contribute to the lives of chil-
dren. There are a many challenges as
there are rewards, however. “If it were
easy working with kids | wouldn't be
doing it," Veneziano said.

To find out about volunteer opportu-
nities with children in Snohomish County
see www.co.snohomish.wa.us/Volun-
teers and for information on education,
training and employment in Washington
state see “Plan for Tomorrow Today : A
Guide to Occupations in Washington
State" at www.wa.gov/careerguide.

The Everett Public Library has a
Career Center where you can discuss
career and education options with a
career specialist. Their Web site is
www.epls.org/CareerCenter and their
phone number is (425) 257-8012.
