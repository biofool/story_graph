Oregon makes bid to attract campers

Once threatened by commercialism, the pris-
tine Oregon Coast has become a 400-mile-long
parkland of foaming surf, giant sea stacks, des-
ert-like dunes, broad beaches and forested capes
that rise high above the sea.

It's no surprise, then, that many Northern Cali-
fornians — myself included — have discovered
this beautiful shoreline and its long string of state
parks.

And, at last, Oregon itself has realized the im-
pact from out-of-state travelers. The state now
bills the area as a friendly haven for tourists and
as an all-season destination.

As of Jan. 1, Oregon has stopped hitting up non-
resident campers with a $2 surcharge. “The extra
fee charged to out-of-state visitors is counterpro-
ductive to Oregon's efforts to promote tourism,”
Gov. Neil Goldschmidt said last year as he signed
the bill repealing the tax.

State officials admitted that the 10-year-old fee
prompted negative reactions outside Oregon. One
state, Washington, had returned the favor by
charging Oregon travelers extra for camping in
its parks.

In addition, more Oregon parks than ever now
remain open year-round.

“Many people know that the weather can be
nearly as pleasant during the off-season as during
the summer on the coast,” one ranger says. “They
fish, hike, camp, dig for clams, watch for whales
and attend some of the festivals that take place in
coastal communities.”

Camping fees, too, have been lowered during
the non-summer months — ranging from $5 for a
tent site to $7 for a full-hookup RV space (in sum-
mer, it’s $8 to $10). Likewise, the usual $1-per-car
entry fee at certain day-use areas is suspended in
winter

maps.

ed-relief, elevation-tinted flat wall map of Califor-
nia, developed by Raven Maps and Images. This
striking map, produced by digital computers, is
designed to be hung on a wall and used for basic,
pre-trip planning.

It features high-quality paper, seven inks and
