Aikido keeps
him balanced

By JANET HOLMAN PARMER
Correspondent

Five years
ago, Teja Bell’s
recordings were
near the top of
the Billboard
charts. He hasn’t
had any big hits
since then, but
Bell, a jazz gui-
tarist and mar-
tial artist, hopes
that’s about to
change.

Bell is prepar-
ing to release three albums in 1994,
including a solo recording, “Snow
Leopard,” that was virtually com-
plete in 1989 when the record deal
fell through.

He’s unfazed by the vagaries of
the music industry, and hopes with
the type of music he plays, known
as contemporary jazz or New Age,
that a few years between releases
shouldn't affect his popularity.

“It doesn’t matter that I was out of
the loop with this kind of music.
With pop music, it might make a
difference,” says Bell, 43, a solidly
built man whose thick, wavy hair
hangs below his shoulders. “But it’s
important to have a new release,
because there’s a certain kind of
feedback that comes back.”

A Petaluma resident since 1990,
Bell's energies are focused in three
directions: composing and perform-
ing music; record engineering and
producing; and training and teach-
ing aikido, a Japanese martial art.

Aikido unleashes creativity

Bell started practicing aikido 23
years ago, and has studied under
top aikido masters in the Bay Area
and Japan. He believes aikido has
helped unleash his creative powers
and enhanced his musical abilities.

Aikido integrates the mind, body
and spirit, Bell says. While it is an
effective self-defense technique, ai-
kido also encourages spiritual
awareness, physical balance and
harmonious movement.

In Petaluma, he’s developed a
loyal following of adults and chil-
dren, who study aikido with him
through Parks and Recreation De-
partment classes, and this month
he’s introducing low-impact aikido
for adults.

For the past 10 years, Bell man-
aged the children’s program at a
Mill Valley aikido school, but ended
his tenure in December to allow
time for other ventures.

Bell says aikido offers children “a
learning paradigm that’s generally
not available in our culture. It’s
learning without competition and

Studio is money-maker

Bell invested the proceeds from
his first album in recording equip-
ment, and says he feels fortunate to
have the studio as a money-maker,
since working as a musician is a
“dubious” way to earn a living.

He also composes music within
the studio using an electronic key-
board and synthesizers hooked up
to a sophisticated music computer
program.

He spent the first week of 1994
wrapping up a recording project
with west Marin poet Robert Hall,
who approached Bell about compos-
ing music to accompany his poems.

“I composed music as a land-
scape, It’s not a rhyming style of
poetry, it’s more stream-of-con-
sciousness, inspirational and intu-
itive,” Bell says.

Raised in a musical family and
trained as a classical guitarist, Bell
distinguished himself as a contem-
porary jazz musician in the late
1980s. “Dolphin Smiles,” an album
he and violinist Steven Kindler
recorded, rose to fifth place on
Billboard’s top 10 contemporary
jazz albums chart in 1988. The
album remained on the music in-
dustry publication’s lists for 35
weeks. Billboard also placed Bell

of Tamalpais Dojo in Mill Valley.
He finds aikido a demanding but

balance with his musical pursuits.
