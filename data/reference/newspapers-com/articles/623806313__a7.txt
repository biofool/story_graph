For more than a century, the Army kept the
mouth of San Francisco Bay well fortified with
weaponry from one generation after another.

Today across the Golden Gate, the big guns are
gone, but the abandoned bunkers and empty
missile silos remain. So, too, do the Marin Coast’s
wave-whipped beaches, protected valleys,
windswept bluffs, and eye-popping views of San
Francisco and the Pacific.

This unique blend of natural and human history
forms the Marin Headlands’ unit of the Golden
Gate National Recreation Area — a place made
for walking. In fact, says one aficionado, the area
offers “an exceptional chance to get away from it
all within sight of one of the world’s greatest
cities.”

A good spot to start your Marin Headlands
adventuring is at the Rodeo Beach visitors’ center
in Rodeo Valley. There, you can bone up on the
area’s past and present, and pick up a map
detailing the trails and fortresses.

If you want solitude, take a backcountry hike,
Two trails, Miwok and Coastal, traverse Wolf
Ridge behind Rodeo Beach. Another, the
Tennessee Valley, ends at a scenic cove.

The hills opposite Rodeo Valley include a
cluster of antique batteries dating from different
eras. You can walk (or drive) there from Rodeo
Beach.

If you join the ranger-led tour, you’ll find out
that the largest of the World War II-era guns fired
a shell the weight of a Volkswagen 26 miles to sea.
Test firing, you'll be told, rattled windows up and
down the coast.

Nearby, another historic attraction, the
19th-century Point Bonita Light Station, is worth a
visit (open weekends). But it’s not for the faint of
heart. To get there, you hike a half-mile trail,
walk through a low rock tunnel and then cross a
swaying suspension bridge 120 feet above the
crashing waves.

Point Bonita originally stood higher on the
bluff, but the Golden Gate’s low-lying mist often
obscured the beacon. One befogged keeper

round. Topics vary and reservations may be

Other Headlands’ activities include picnicking
and camping (hike-in style). Nearby, Mount
Tamalpais State Park offers “en route” sites for
RVers and walk-in sites for tent campers.

To reach the Marin Headlands from San
Francisco, exit at Alexander Avenue off U.S. 101.
From the north, take the last exit before crossing
the bridge. There are two park entrances: one
along cliffside Conzelman Road (terrific
panoramas show the Golden Gate with San
Francisco as a backdrop) and the other via a
tunnel. Both routes lead to Rodeo Valley.

During your Headlands visit, watch your step
on the cliffs (poor footing) and in the bunkers
(broken glass).

Mountain images — Often in his travels,
noted wilderness photojournalist Galen Rowell
emits the battle cry, “Let’s unset the sunset!” He
then hikes, runs or drives to higher ground, racing
the shadow of last light.

“A photographer who deals with two or three
sunsets a day,” he says, “has more chances to be
‘lucky’ than a person who settles for just one.”

That's only one of many nuggets to be found in
Rowell’s highly acclaimed “Mountain Light,”
which has just been released in paperback (Sierra
Club; $19.95). The beautiful, large-format book
showcases 80 of his finest color images.

In his accompanying text, Rowell covers the
stories behind the pictures, relates his early days
of picture taking, discusses the art of mountain
photography and offers specific tips for outdoor
shooters. Here’s a sampling:

® Magic light: “Most people think of landscapes
simply as objects to be photographed. When the
magic hour arrives (the hour at either end of the
day, when colors of light mix together like a
kaleidoscope), my thoughts center on light rather
than on the landscape. I search for perfect light,
then hunt for something earthbound to match it
with.”

e After hours: “Many of my favorite images
were made after sunset or before sunrise. To
work only in direct light is to miss a feat of subtle
twilight colors that often become far more vivid
on film than they appear to the eye.”

© Overcast day: “As soon as I see that shadows
are gone, I know I have soft light and a contrast
ratio well within the limits of my film. I can
photograph faces without fear of having them go
partially black. I can shoot forest detail that
would otherwise be splotched with light and dark
spots. I also look for the colors of flowers and
meadows to come alive.”

© Tripods: “For a wilderness photographer,
lightness is a more significant quality than
sturdiness at full extension. When I need
increased stability, | compromise: I get down on
my knees and use my lightweight tripod without
its legs extended.”
