The two white-clad figures come toward each
other and bow from the waist — keeping watchful
eyes on each other, The scene freezes, until the small
woman catapults a kick to her opponent's head.

“It's speed and concentration that give you
power,” she explains after the ‘‘empty-hand’’ or kar-
ate sparring has ended with polite bows. ‘And ac-
curacy,’ she adds. ‘“‘You have to know where to
punch , . . Your body is relaxed, then you speed up
and concentrate all your strength.”’

Hedwig Tourangeau is the first to admit that she
doesn’t fit the stereotype that people have of karate
students.

Soft-spoken, she explains that karate has helped
her overcome shyness, and it has given her a strong-
er, well-coordinated body. She feels ready to tackle
new activities, even karate competition. Until you try,
“you don’t realize how far your body can go,” she
says. ‘‘The worst I can do is lose,”’

“You become more modest, a little more grate-
ful,’ she adds after a pause.

Clap, clap. Sixteen white-clad figures kneeling on
the mat draw the attention of the gods before step-
ping into a world of smooth, apparently effortless
sparring. Well, it’s effortless for some, while others
work up a tangle of arms and legs.

Under the eye of the “‘sensei,’’ or teacher, the
students work in pairs. The attacker rushes to grab
his partner, who sidesteps, takes him off balance,
spins him around and throws him down with a merci-
less pin of the wrist and arm. The attacker rolls and
rushes back for more action. The two are joined by
the same flow of energy. That’s aikido, also called
dancing martial art.

Tourangeau and the aikido students are among a
growing number of dedicated martial artists sweating
to learn the ‘‘way of perfection,”’ ‘‘path of harmoniz-
ing energy,” ‘gentle way of fighting’’ or the ‘‘way of
hands and feet.”’

Behind the poetry of translation hide kung fu, ai-
kido, judo and taekwando a few of the imported
Asian martial arts that have taken hold in the land of
the gun.

Love-fear feelings link the public to martial art-
ists, who are envied as supermen and superwomen,
but often are misunderstood: The wise won't come
too close ... You don’t go up to a black belt in karate
and ask him why he puts on pajamas to fight, or
what’s the point of breaking bricks with a hand, feet
or forehead.

Even martial arts students don’t know what they
are getting into.

At first, ‘they try although they don’t know
why,” explains Shigeki Umemoto, a karate, judo and
kendo (sword) expert who started a martial arts
school in Great Falls 10 years ago.

Describing the learning process, he explains:
“First you suffer, then you try hard ... so you will
succeed eventually.”’

Most people don’t understand what ‘suffer’
means, he adds: It’s part of growing. Nobody can do
it for you.

After a while students start understanding what
they are doing. It’s like ‘‘you drink wine, and wine
drinks you,’’ says Umemoto.

Marshal arts were a mysterious and flashy form
of self-defense in the eyes of a youngster, recalls
Steve Capener, 22, a karate third-degree black belt in-
structor at Umemoto's school, or ‘‘dojo.'’ He was
drawn into the art when he was in high school, Cap-
ener and his friend, Mark Anderson, started together
and stumbled on to the hard truth fast.

“The mystique kind of disappears and it just be-
comes hard work,’’ says Anderson, 23. Now a second-
degree black belt, he teaches a karate ‘center
course” at the University of Montana.
