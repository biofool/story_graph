In most aikido schools, the
emphasis does not fall on
self-defense, but by its nature
aikido cultivates skills that
might aid in dispelling or side-
stepping circumstances of
actual or potential violence —
mobility, relaxation, awareness
and a sensitivity to the energy
of people and situations. “The
real aikido,” a famous teacher
told me years ago, “is how you
deal with people.”

Because it is not a retaliatory
system, and by dint of the reci-
procity the practice involves,
aikido cultivates a preference
for conflict resolution over
combative posturing. And
because it embraces “10,000”
techniques — a figure in Asian
cultures implying “unnumber-
ed” — practitioners can find
multiple ways to get out of
situations leading to confronta-
tion.

Longtime practitioners of
aikido learn to control the
panic that occurs when some-
one abruptly invades their
space, a practice called, by my
former teacher, the “re-educa-

tion of instinct.” °

Much of martial arts tradi-
tion, in Japan and elsewhere,
has centered on the idea of
counterattack, of punishing
someone who tries to do you
harm.

Ueshiba — known to stu-
dents of aikido as O Sensei, or
“ereat teacher” — immersed
himself in various martial
disciplines from a young age.
He came to understand that
already in his time the world
had become too small for retal-
iation. It could lead only to
endless cycles of violent retri-
bution such as we see in gang

. turf wars and in countless

other instances,

At heart, the student of
aikido strives to realize that
anyone who would inflict
violence must themselves be
afflicted, and we have no ex-
cuse for punishing the afflict-
ed, I say “at heart,” because
while we may readily recog-
nize the correctness of this
ethical posture, internalizing it
does not come easy, if ever.
And the essence of aikido
practice, in one view, is inter-
