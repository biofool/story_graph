Advice for over-50
aikido practitioners

Being vigorously physical in
nature, Aikido’s endless trial-
and-error process can involve
injuries.

But in nearly 25 years of
practice, I have suffered noth-
ing more serious than a
sprained finger. So I asked my
teacher, Penny Sablove, a
fourth-degree black belt, chief
instructor at Heart of San
Francisco Aikido and a phys-
ical therapist by trade, wheth-
er she thinks injuries in aikido
are inevitable.

“Injuries are not inevitable
in aikido,” she said in an e-
mail. “In my experience, the
kinesthetic and interpersonal
sensitivity people cultivate in
aikido goes a long way toward
keeping the practice safe. Ai-
kido requires being in the
moment, as opposed to on
automatic pilot. For that rea-
son, when something doesn’t
feel quite right in the practitio-
ner’s body or with the situa-
tion, she/he is likely to adapt
on the spot. ...

“On a larger level,” she
added, “you could say that all
physical activities involve
risks, but there are also seri-
ous risks to inactivity.”

When I asked for her advice
to people continuing aikido or
beginning to study it after 50,
Sablove said, “I believe that
the best option for everyone is
to begin their aikido training
in an introductory class,
where everything is slowed
down and extensively ex-
plained. It is also very impor-
tant to inform your instructor

1 ws

of any known limitations — a
back condition, a previous
knee surgery, ete — at the
onset. Since older adults are
more vulnerable to injury,
with longer healing times, it is
best to avoid repeating any
new skill over and over. Do ita
few times and then see how
you feel within the next 48
hours. In this way, people can
learn where the limits are,
instead of either exceeding
them or under-doing.”

And for someone who
would resume practicing later
in life, after a hiatus like mine?

“People over 50 returning to
Aikido will face an interesting
challenge; their nervous sys-
tems will remember a lot of
the movements, but their bod-
ies will not really be condi-
tioned to perform them. So
restarting in an introductory
class or taking frequent
breaks during a regular class
are some good options.”

Sablove also mentioned one
of the hardest lessons for aiki-
do practitioners of any age: “to
resist comparing themselves
to others. Everyone will pick
up some aspects of aikido
quickly and struggle with
others. It is common to be
blind to our strengths and to
dwell on the areas of difficulty
and in this way, people can
hold themselves back. For
people over 50, it will be espe-
cially important to follow the
dictates of one’s own body and
feel good about the fact that
they are practicing.”

— Kenneth Baker
