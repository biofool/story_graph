All this may sound unlikely if you’re
unfamiliar with aikido or related mar-
tial arts such as t’ai chi, but it is similar
to what happens in any art during the
creative moment. Just as the attacker
and the attacked become one, swept up
in a single spiral of movement, so the
painter is one with his brush, the
drummer with his traps, the writer
with his pen, allowing free flow of
energy to cut a unique and surprising
course

Sculptor Richard Kamler is one
among many artists who find that they
achieve this heightened presence and
creative flow more effectively in a
situation that is intense or dangerous.
It is for this reason that he chose to
teach in the maximum security prison
of San Quentin.

Kamler thinks that many of the
prisoners are behind bars because they
never learned successful ways of man-
aging conflict. He overheard one of
them talk about getting a gun as soon
as he got out — so his wife would let
him see their baby! This man is not
likely to stay out of jail long unless he
learns that there are choices other than
violence open to him.

But what goes for the habitual
violent criminal also goes to a large
extent for the chronic victim, the fellow
who asks: Why does it always happen
to me?

There is sometimes an answer. Not
always, of course, because there are no
guarantees and there is no absolute
protection against anything. But more
often than it might appear, there is an
answer to the question: Why me? It
revolves around presence. 0
