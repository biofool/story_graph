© people who work in dan-
gerous situations (policemen,
firemen, bus drivers and cab-
bies, among others). knowing how to
scan — a by-product of presence —
may mean the difference between life
and death. To those who work with
confined people (in prisons, locked
psychiatric wards, schools), it is a key
to effectiveness. To everyone, it can
‘mean the difference between the self-
imprisonment of overcaution and a
free, creative life.

Without presence, the most elabo-
rate security measures can be worse
than useless. Window bars installed to
keep out burglars can become fire-
traps, guns bought for home protection
can end up being shot at children.
Unlike such security equipment, pres-
ence cannot be purchased. It is some-
thing one either grows up with natural-
ly or learns through experience or the
study of various intuitive arts that
require coordination between the mind
and the body.

Among these is aikido, a martial art
that calls for blending with the energy
of the attacker and redirecting the
attack in such a way that nobody gets
hurt. The name translates from the
Japanese as “energy harmony way.”
One of its most creative teachers is
Robert Nadeau, a former policeman.

As a cop in Redwood City, Nadeau
knew officers who seemed to have an
uncanny ability to read between lines.
“There were some guys, if there was a
bank robbery or something, they would
go to the strangest intersections,” he
recalled in an interview at Aikido of
San Francisco. “And lo and behold,
here come the bandits! One guy used to
be able to do it almost every time and
there was no rhyme or reason, he just
had a hunch.”

When he started to study aikido,
Nadeau discovered that this uncanny

intuition could be cultivated. For he
too started having such hunches. “I’d
see a parked car and I just had a sense
of something wrong so I'd look and I'd
see kids hiding because they had stolen
something. This happened quite a few
times, that kind of thing, and I knew it
was from aikido.”

Eventually, his growing interest in
the art led him to Japan where he
studied with its founder, Morihei
Ueshiba, who was tiny and old but
could dispatch several hulking young
attackers, head over heels, all at once
and without so much as touching them.
After several years of intensive prac-
tice, Nadeau returned, convinced that
the essence of aikido — presence under
pressure — could be available to any-
one and did not necessarily require
long years of training.

The people studying with him and
the growing number of other aikido
teachers in the Bay area — where the
art is most strongly established in the
U.S. — are a diverse collection that
includes musicians, lawyers, physi-
cians, therapists, gardeners and engi-
neers. All who were asked were able to
tell of incidents where what they
learned on the mat served them well
during a crisis. A jewelry store manag-
er, a relative novice, used a simple
aikido move to divert a hammer blow
from a customer who attacked him as
he was squatting before the safe, about
to open it. A woman said she was able
to repel a knife-wielding stranger who
tried to force his way into her apart-
ment house in the Haight as she was
about to enter.

An experienced student at the
black-belt level, Steve Gengo, recalled
several occasions during his work as
psychiatric technician at the McAuley
Neuropsychiatric Institute in St.
Mary’s Hospital when he was able to
control violent patients without using

He has discovered that when he
approaches violent patients with a
sense of presence that includes an
acceptence of them, they calm down
almost immediately. “There is nothing
for them to fight against,” he ex-
plained: :

All this may sound unlikely if you’re
unfamiliar with aikido or related mar-
tial arts such as t’ai chi, but it is similar
to what happens in any art during the
creative moment. Just as the attacker
and the attacked become one, swept up
in a single spiral of movement, so the
painter. is one with his brush, the
drummer with his traps, the writer
with his pen, allowing free flow of
energy to cut a unique and surprising
course.

Sculptor Richard Kamler is one
among many artists who find that they
achieve this heightened presence and
creative flow more effectively in a
situation that is intense or dangerous.
It is for this reason that he chose to
teach in the maximum security prison
of San Quentin.

Kamler thinks that many of the
prisoners are behind bars because they
never learned successful ways of man-
aging conflict. He overheard one of
them talk about getting a gun as soon
as he got out — so his wife would let
him see their baby! This man is not
likely to stay out of jail long unless he
learns that there are choices other than
violence open to him.

But what goes for the habitual
violent criminal also goes to a large
extent for the chronic victim, the fellow
who asks: Why does it always happen
to me?

There is sometimes an answer. Not
always, of course, because there are no
guarantees and there is no absolute
protection against anything. But more
often than it might appear, there is an
answer to the question: Why me? It
revolves around presence. 0
