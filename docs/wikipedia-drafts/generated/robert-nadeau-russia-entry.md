## Proposed Wikipedia Section

### Seminars in the Soviet Union and Russia

Robert Nadeau conducted aikido seminars in the Soviet Union during the late 1980s and early 1990s, visiting the country at the invitation of Soviet aikido practitioners. According to the Moscow Aiki Club (aikiclub.ru), Nadeau — then holding the rank of 6th dan — visited the Soviet Union and conducted a training session at the Lenkai club in Leningrad on October 27, 1990, the day of the founding conference of the USSR Aikido Federation.<ref name="aikiclub"/>

Sergey Kiselev, founder of the Lenkai Aikido Club in Leningrad and the first President of the USSR Aikido Federation, documents the broader context in his first-person history of aikido in the USSR: the first All-Union Aikido Seminar was held in Moscow in January 1990, bringing together delegates from across the USSR and leading to the creation of the All-Union Commission for Aikido Development under the USSR State Sports Committee.<ref name="lenkai"/>

The Kazan Aiki Club, founded in 1987, documented that its practitioners traveled to international aikido seminars in Moscow, Leningrad, Vladivostok, Khabarovsk, Kiev, Minsk, Baku, and other Soviet cities from 1987 to 1994, and trained with Nadeau alongside other prominent international instructors including Jack Wada (7th dan, USA), Christian Tissier (7th dan, France), and Koichi Berrish (USA).<ref name="bujutsu"/><ref name="rebenok"/>

Nadeau's visit occurred within a broader pattern of American aikido instructors visiting the USSR. Koichi Barrish was the first American aikido instructor to visit Moscow, in 1987, and conducted annual seminars through the mid-1990s.<ref name="kannagara"/> Jamie Zimron Sensei confirms she "helped bring Aikido to the former Soviet Union" starting in 1987 with Barrish, continuing until the Soviet Union fell in 1991.<ref name="zimron"/>

Nadeau's influence on aikido in Russia is noted by multiple sources, including his own dojo's biography which states he "profoundly influenced generations of Aikido teachers in America, Europe, Russia, Israel, and New Zealand."<ref name="cityaikido"/>

### Key evidence from sources

>
> Translation: "On October 27, 1990, the day of the founding conference of the USSR Aikido Federation, Robert Nadeau (6th dan, USA), who visited the Soviet Union at the invitation of Moscow aikido practitioners, conducted a training at the Lenkai club."

**lenkai.spb.ru** (Sergey Kiselev, Lenkai Club founder and USSR Aikido Federation President):
> Documents the first All-Union Aikido Seminar in Moscow (January 1990) and the founding of the USSR Aikido Federation. Kiselev is the central figure in Soviet aikido history — his account is the closest thing to an official history.

**bujutsu.ru** (Tatarstan Bujutsu Federation):
> "тренироваться у таких известных мастеров Айкидо, как Джек Вада (7 Дан Aikido Aikikai США), Роберт Надо (6 Дан Aikido Aikikai, Канада), Кристиан Тисьер (7 Дан Aikido Aikikai, Франция)"
>
> Translation: "train with such famous Aikido masters as Jack Wada (7th dan, USA), Robert Nadeau (6th dan, Aikido Aikikai, Canada), Christian Tissier (7th dan, Aikido Aikikai, France)"

Note: "Канада" (Canada) is likely an error — Nadeau is American.

**kannagara-aikido.ru** (Koichi Barrish bio):
> "Впервые посетил Москву в 1987г. До середины 90-х ежегодно проводил семинары в Москве."
>
> Translation: "First visited Moscow in 1987. Conducted annual seminars in Moscow through the mid-1990s."

**bigrock-aikikai.com** (Jamie Zimron interview):
> "Jamie Sensei even helped bring Aikido to the former Soviet Union at a time when martial arts had been outlawed through communism. In 1987, Jamie Sensei and thirty others travelled with Rev. Koichi Barrish Sensei, training dangerously underground, and she continued to lead American training groups as the head instructor until the Soviet Union fell in 1991."

### Caveats

1. **No independent secondary journalistic source** has been found in any archive. The strongest evidence remains organizational/archival Russian sources and the Lenkai club history.

2. The Russian sources (aikiclub.ru, lenkai.spb.ru, bujutsu.ru) are organizational martial arts sites, not independent journalism. They document historical facts but lack editorial oversight in the Wikipedia WP:RS sense.

3. The aikiclub.ru source gives the exact date (October 27, 1990) and context (founding conference of the USSR Aikido Federation) — this is the most precise evidence found.

4. The Lenkai source (lenkai.spb.ru) is significant because Kiselev was the central figure in Soviet aikido — his first-person account is the closest thing to an official history. However, it is still an organizational/first-person source.

5. Per Wikipedia's WP:RS guidelines, these MARGINAL sources can support the factual claim with attribution ("according to...") but not in Wikipedia's unqualified voice.
